Forecasts of Post-COVID Pulse Prices in the Global South

data-science
R, Forecasting, Gradient Boosting
Published

January 1, 2021

Summary:

Pulses have been considered an environmentally-friendly and inexpensive alternative to commodities like meat, ideal in helping to combat food insecurity. The FFPI (FAO Food Price Index— measure of international food commodity prices) suggests that effects of the COVID-19 pandemic have caused dramatic price surges in all food groups, including pulses, that threaten our ability to feed the growing global population. But is it really just the impacts of the pandemic that threatens this ability?

In order to answer the ultimate question of: “How did observed pulse prices differ from pre-pandemic price dynamics during COVID-19?”, I wanted to focus on the price forecasts of this food group in countries most susceptible to food insecurity as determined by the World Food Programme (WFP). I examine food price data from the WFP to create time series forecasts via gradient boosting of what could have happened to the prices of select pulses in the developing world if the pandemic had not occurred.

Tip🫘 Key Takeaways
  • Forecasts predicted overall lower pricing for Pulses, suggesting the Pandemic may have triggered a series of unobserved events resulting in actual prices rising more than expected
  • Cyclical behaviors occurring in both forecasts and actual pricing suggest that there are deeper long term issues that need to be addressed in order to maintain viability of pulses as a food source.

Methods:

  • Nested Cross Validation

Background

Food Security & The Environment

Food production, food security, and climate change are inexplicably linked. According to the WFP,

“ The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change in its 5th Assessment Report is clear: the advent of more frequent and intense disasters caused by climate change could negatively affect people’s ability to produce, access and consume food. “*

Ultimately impacting the price instability of food and putting the food security of vulnerable communities at risk. Thus, we must establish urgent and sustainable measures to ease pressure on agricultural ecosystems.

To read more about the 5th Assessment Report: PCC. 2015. Climate change 2014: Synthesis report. Geneva, Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change.*

One quarter of the world’s greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions come from food production. So it stands to reason that a possible start to a solution for food insecurity exacerbated by climate change would be shifting our focus to alternative food sources that emit less greenhouse gases into our atmosphere. As shown by the chart above, meat & dairy products like beef (beef herd), cheese & beef (dairy herd) emit large amounts of CO2 into the atmosphere. In comparison, Peas, other pulses, and groundnuts emit much lower amounts of CO2.

The Potential of Pulses

As the previous chart suggests, pulses could help fight food insecurity in vulnerable areas. For those unfamiliar, Pulses are a type of legume that is limited to crops harvested solely for dry grain, thereby excluding leguminous crops harvested green for food, crops used mainly for oil extraction, or crops that are used exclusively for sowing purposes.

The potential of pulses lie in their ability to be nutritious, inexpensive, and environmentally-friendly.

  • They are vital sources of protein and micronutrients like amino acids, iron and zinc, making them a great alternative for GHG emitters like meat and dairy. As protein and energy deficiencies are often the culprit for widespread malnutrition, this makes them an even more ideal solution.
  • They are significantly less expensive than animal proteins, making them more accessible to marginalized communities. They also have a long shelf life, meaning they produce very little food waste, if any, and can be stored for long periods of time without losing their nutrition.
  • The broad genetic diversity of these leguminous crops allow for the selection of climate-resilient varieties and their nitrogen fixing ability improves soil quality and produces a low carbon footprint

The Reality of 2020 - 2021: COVID-19 Pandemic and its Effect on the Food Supply

Fast forward to 2021 and our world is dealing with a series of unprecedented events: a deadly pandemic and climate disasters that are becoming increasingly more extreme, just to name a few.

This does not help set the stage towards improving global food security. According to the World Bank:

“even before COVID-19 reduced incomes and disrupted supply chains, chronic and acute hunger were on the rise due to various factors including conflict, socio-economic conditions, natural hazards, climate change and pests. COVID-19 impacts have led to severe and widespread increases in global food insecurity, affecting vulnerable households in almost every country, with impacts expected to continue through 2021, into 2022, and possibly beyond.”

Pulse Prices Surge

One of the most visible effects of the pandemic for many consumers have been the price surges of goods including pulses. This has been most visible in countries like India and Bangladesh where a majority of their populations rely on a pulses as plant-sourced protein. As these countries have historically struggled with producing enough supply to satisfy domestic demand, they often supplement this by importing large quantities of pulses. The pandemic’s effects like disrupted supply chains and increased demand for goods have only aggravated a huge weakness in global food production.

In April of 2021, India experienced sudden price increases of their top two most consumed pulses: chana (gram) and tur (pigeon peas) going from 100 Rp per kg to 130 Rp per kg causing the Indian government to remove quantitative restrictions on the import of pulses and signing contracts with various countries in order to import even more supply.

In Bangladesh’s largest market, prices of imported pulses from Australia and Canada surge Tk15 a kg in 2 weeks which many importers claimed was a result of the increase of booking price of all pulses in the international market

FAO Food Price Indices

Source: FAO

Pulses are not the only food group experiencing price surges. Like the World Bank’s report suggested, all food commodity prices surged over the course of the past year and a half. The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) body of the UN has been tracking such price changes with their food price and food commodity price indices. Food prices experienced minor fluctuations throughout 2018 and 2019 but for the most part stayed stable. The end of 2020 through 2021 is where the biggest changes in the FPI occur. We can see from the graph on the right that while all tracked commodities experienced increases in pricing, none experienced a more dramatic increase than vegetable oils and sugar.

Original Data

The data I chose to analyze to answer my question about ‘How did observed pulse prices differ from pre-pandemic price dynamics during COVID-19?’ is the World Food Programme’s collection of food price data from 78 different countries that they operate in. While this global prices dataset covers the prices of over 465 different products in 1759 markets from 1992-2018, the WFP did not collect the price data of all the same products from each country at the same time. Thus, there is some disconnect between countries. However, rather than focus on a single country and price data over a limited time, I hope that I can use this dataset to gain a greater understanding of the behavior of price over the most vulnerable parts of the world as a whole over a longer period of time. To get a better picture of the data, I have joined this dataset with datasets about the geographic location of the included markets and commodity category data that will help us focus in on our food group of interest. Both of these datasets have also been collated by the WFP.

Tip🫘 Quick Facts
  • WFP’s record of food prices in 78 countries that they service
  • 901,179 records from 1992-2018
  • Prices of over 465 unique products in 1759 different markets

Methodology

In order to examine the question of “How did observed pulse prices differ from pre-pandemic price dynamics during COVID-19?”, I will use:

  • Google BigQuery to transform dataset to that of just Pulse price data and extract time series
  • Google Data Studio to create visualizations of data
  • R Studio (Ver 4.1.0) to perform time series forecasting with a gradient boosted tree model to visualize what could have happened to prices in 2019 - 2021 given data that ended in 2018.

Masur Lentils (Retail) - Bangladesh

Forecast:

In the visualization below, I include both the full data + forecast (left) and a zoom into the forecast (right)

Masur Lentils price data was observed in Southeast Asian states like Bangladesh, India & Pakistan. The above forecast is based on price data from 6 markets in Bangladesh from October 2005 to August 2018. The September 2018 to August 2021 forecast was created using 3 validation windows of 24 months each, separated by 24 months.

Actual:

This is what actually happened to masur lentil prices in Bangladesh from October 2018 to October 2021. As I expected to see in most of the graphs of actual prices, the 36 month time series shows prices on an upward slope, increasing especially around mid 2020 and continuing into and through 2021. The average price of masur lentils hits a peak right around April-May 2020 at 85 & 84 BDT respectively. (A level we do not see the price hit again until October 2021) This peak happens to occur around the same time that the FAO’s FFPI begins to show rapid increase in price changes of food. This is different from what was forecasted, where the peak begins mid 2019 and does not recover till mid 2020 where it proceeds to stay pretty level at around 65 BDT for the rest of he forecast.

Beans (Retail) - Burundi

Forecast:

The above forecast is based on prices from 8 different markets in Burundi from January 2003 to September 2018. The October 2018 to September 2021 forecast was constructed using 4 validation windows of 24 months separated by 30 months.

Actual:

Again, we see an upwards trend in the actual retail prices of beans in Burundi. Both forecast and actual prices experiencing multiple dips the first being around mid 2019 and the last being in prices starting in early 2021 and begin to recover from it August/ September 2021. In fact, it seems like the forecast captured the same seasonality and cycles that occur in the actual price of beans just at a lower level. The forecast ranged between 1000 to 1300 BIF with the first dip hitting about 750 BIF and the last dip hitting an absolute low of about 670. The actual prices ranged from 1000 to 1650 BIF with the first dip hitting a low of about 850 BIF and the last dip hitting about 950 BIF.

Chickpeas (Retail) - Tajikistan

Forecast:

Chickpea retail price data was observed in Libya, the State of Palestine, the Syrian Arab Republic, Tajikistan and Turkey. This forecast is based on prices from 5 markets in Tajikistan from May 2016 to August 2018. Constructed using 3 validation windows, each 8 months long, separated by 2 months.

Actual

As one can see, the actual prices of chickpeas in Tajikistan behaved very differently from what the forecast predicted. Conclusion

Key Takeaways

  • Forecasts predicted overall lower pricing for Pulses, suggesting the Pandemic may have triggered a series of unobserved events resulting in actual prices rising more than expected
  • Cyclical behaviors occurring in both forecasts and actual pricing suggest that there are deeper long term issues that need to be addressed in order to maintain viability of pulses as a food source.

While as we predicted, prices of pulses could have been consistently lower if the pandemic had not occurred, the forecasts picked up cyclical behaviors that (for the most part) also occurred in the plots of actual prices. A further study could be done in what was already occurring in pulse production that is causing the cyclical behavior as that could give a better indication of long term issues in pulse production that threaten its potential.
